01 / Curriculum and Academic Planning

Connecting courses into a coherent pathway

Teachers and school leaders need a shared view of what students have learned, what comes next, and where gaps or unnecessary repetition may occur.

I led Grades 7–12 mathematics vertical-alignment work and coordinated curriculum conversations across departments. The evidence collection includes mathematics, science, English language arts, and social studies alignment materials, alongside an Academic Council template linking priority standards, unit plans, and grade-to-grade progression.

I also developed a working Upper School course catalog to bring graduation requirements, course descriptions, prerequisites, and advanced study pathways into one reference for students and families. The current catalog connects those pathways to the school's academic policies.

Evidence: alignment workbooks, departmental course descriptions, Academic Council curriculum overview template, and the 2026–27 course catalog. The catalog remains an internal working draft; departmental work is collaborative rather than attributed to one author.

Explore the curriculum review framework

Adapted Portfolio Example

  1. Identify priority standards and intended learning.
  2. Link the standards to units and formative and summative assessments.
  3. Make prerequisite skills and next-course readiness visible.
  4. Examine gaps, duplication, and transitions with department colleagues.
  5. Record decisions and connect them to course planning and student guidance.

The template links to existing planning documents rather than asking teachers to rewrite their curriculum.

02 / Teacher Growth and Instructional Coaching

From observation as an event to growth as a process

Observation is most useful when it connects a teacher's goals to classroom evidence, reflection, and a manageable next step.

Building on my observation and feedback work, I am co-developing a whole-school Growth Partner and peer-observation framework with school colleagues. The design combines a chosen school goal, an individual professional goal, announced Growth Partner observations, pre- and post-conferences, and teacher-selected peer partners.

The peer process is designed around collegial learning rather than performance ratings. Teachers identify a focus, record evidence of student learning, discuss what worked, and choose something to try next.

Evidence: whole-school observation framework, peer observation and reflection form, and teacher goal-setting and reflection form. Status: development and rollout; impact results are not yet established.

Explore the Growth Partner and peer-learning cycle

Framework Design

  1. Choose one shared school goal and one professional goal, with an optional personal goal.
  2. Agree on the observation focus and the evidence to look for.
  3. Use announced Growth Partner observations with pre- and post-conferences.
  4. Choose peer colleagues; each teacher conducts and receives two peer observations annually.
  5. Connect reflection and follow-up to the teacher's goals.

The design provides one Growth Partner observation per quarter. The aim is collective professional growth, not another compliance exercise.

03 / AP Assessment and Academic Evidence

Looking beyond classroom grades

A strong course grade and readiness for an externally assessed examination are related questions, but they are not the same question.

I developed a grades-versus-AP-exam diagnostic to bring internal course performance and external examination results into the same discussion. The work draws on College Board instructional planning reports and a subject-level comparison workbook, helping identify where grading, curriculum, assessment, and readiness deserve closer review.

A separate AP monitoring template brings together course grades, mock examinations, predicted outcomes, final scores, and intervention notes. Its purpose is to make evidence usable for teachers and leaders rather than produce another disconnected report.

Evidence: College Board instructional planning reports, the grades-versus-exam comparison workbook, and an AP performance-tracker template. The diagnostic is evidence of leadership practice, not proof that a single tool caused changes in examination results. Student-level data and identifiable small-cohort results are not reproduced here.

Explore the AP review questions

Adapted Portfolio Example

  1. Which examination year, course, cohort, and exam form does each result describe?
  2. How many students enrolled, how many took the examination, and which denominator is being used?
  3. Where do course grades and examination outcomes differ enough to merit review?
  4. What do subject-level strengths and gaps suggest about curriculum, rigor, and support?
  5. What action will the department take, and what evidence will be reviewed next?

Comparisons should distinguish students from examinations and separate a documented outcome from an unsupported claim of causation.

04 / Faculty Professional Learning

Turning teacher language into shared practice

Professional learning should help teachers do something useful in their next lesson, not simply introduce another framework.

Working with colleagues, I developed bilingual Responsive Classroom teacher-language materials for Upper School professional learning. English and Spanish presentations, collaborative readings, and practice notes connect reinforcing, reminding, redirecting, and envisioning language to classroom situations.

The materials combine discussion and rehearsal so teachers can examine the language they use, consider its effect on student independence, and identify a concrete change to try.

Evidence: English and Spanish training decks, bilingual jigsaw materials, and teacher practice notes. These demonstrate the design and support for professional learning; classroom impact requires follow-up evidence.

Explore the professional-learning sequence

Adapted Portfolio Example

  1. Read and discuss a focused teacher-language approach.
  2. Examine an example from an Upper School classroom.
  3. Rehearse a clearer or more purposeful response.
  4. Choose a situation in which to try the approach.
  5. Return to evidence of student response and reflect with colleagues.

This is an application of Responsive Classroom practices, not a claim to have originated the framework.

05 / Student Support and Academic Monitoring

Making missing work visible and follow-up consistent

Missing assignments can accumulate before a student receives coordinated support. A useful intervention system needs both clear information and a practical response.

I created the Zero Zeros initiative to identify missing work, organize student conversations, set reduction goals, and connect students with academic support. The work expanded from an initial grade-level focus to monitoring across Grades 7–12.

The intended review cycle preserves week-to-week history rather than overwriting the previous position, making it possible to see whether follow-up is helping.

Evidence: dated academic-monitoring workbooks and intervention structures. Status: ongoing implementation; verified outcome comparisons are still being developed. Student names, grades, and case notes remain private.

Explore the intervention cycle

Adapted Portfolio Example

  1. Identify missing work from current gradebook evidence.
  2. Meet with the student to understand the barriers.
  3. Agree on a manageable completion goal and available support.
  4. Coordinate expectations with the relevant teachers.
  5. Review progress against the previous week's position.

06 / Responsible AI Leadership

Turning emerging technology into clear expectations

Schools need practical guidance on how AI can support learning while protecting academic integrity and professional judgment.

I helped lead a cross-department AI committee and developed bilingual policy language addressing student disclosure, teacher-authorized use, privacy, review of AI-generated materials, and responses to academic misuse. The work translated broad principles into expectations for daily school practice.

Evidence: bilingual policy-change summary and supporting policy research. This case focuses on policy development rather than a claim of measured implementation impact.

Explore the policy framework

Adapted Portfolio Example

  • Set clear expectations for permitted assistance and disclosure.
  • Require students to explain and demonstrate their own understanding.
  • Keep teachers responsible for reviewing and adapting materials.
  • Protect student and staff information.
  • Use a clear review process when concerns arise.
